# AI Use and Ethics Policy

This document describes how AI systems were used in the Automation Structures research. The opinions and positions taken here are my own and do not represent those of any organization or party. AI use and ethics is an evolving topic, and a fuller treatment is larger than this document warrants.

The Automation Structures research project intentionally and deliberately uses AI extensively throughout. It was spawned out of an experiment in AI-Human collaboration that started in January 2025. I wanted to use AI as an assistant to build the automation platform that I wanted to have as a practitioner. In order to build what I had envisioned I needed to answer several questions and the resulting research project is an attempt to answer them.

Where I draw the line personally with ethical AI use is in human-to-human communication, or content. When the human thinking is taken out of the equation the result becomes soulless. [Don't paste the AI, please](https://dontpastetheai.com/) sums it up rather well and I think a consensus is forming that it is kind of rude to replace human communication with AI. I drive AI tools in a variety of ways to extend capability but not to replace the human element of thinking. Contrast that with technical documentation like a man page, which is more descriptive of a product and less a vehicle for expressing ideas from me as a person.

What I am after is authenticity, not authorship over every line. I want the work to reflect my thinking, judgment, and intent, and I want to be honest about how it was produced. How much of it I write myself depends on the purpose of the material.

To make that distinction concrete, I group the material in this project into three categories. These categories describe the purpose of the material and how it was produced. They are not rankings of quality or importance.

| Category | Kind of content | My role | AI involvement |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Human-to-human communication | My ideas, experiences, judgments, or opinions, communicated from me as a person to the reader as a person. It may discuss technical subjects, but its purpose is not merely to describe a thing. | I originate the ideas and write the content. | Proofreading only. AI may identify or correct spelling and grammar, but it does not contribute ideas or substantive wording. |
| 2. Collaborative research and technical writing | Writing that combines my ideas and judgments with technical explanation or description. | The ideas and direction are mine, and I am the primary contributor to the technical writing. I review and accept the final result. | Collaborative. AI may help me explore or challenge ideas and may help structure, draft, or edit the technical material. |
| 3. Technical artifacts and reference material | Material whose purpose is to describe, implement, or verify a thing. This includes code, formal specifications and proofs, manuals, and reference documentation. | I set the objectives, architecture, constraints, and acceptance criteria. I review what is produced and remain accountable for what is accepted. | AI is the primary producer. It creates much of the material under my direction, subject to the appropriate review, testing, or machine checking. |

I fundamentally do not trust any generated output from AI. And the inconsistency in generated solutions is problematic if the goal is to construct something reliable. That is the primary reason behind the formal verification work. It provides the mechanism for validating the results. Generating solutions from scratch leads to drift from specifications and a host of other problems. However, the tendency for AI to pattern match has been especially valuable in this research when searching for patterns in existing solutions.

The ways I have utilized AI tooling depend on the task and shift around, but my development process sits between [Agentic Engineering](https://simonwillison.net/guides/agentic-engineering-patterns/) and [Fences](https://yegge.ai/essays/fences-not-sandboxes/). Willison's patterns cover delegating implementation to coding agents while keeping human judgment over architecture and quality. Yegge argues that capable agents should be governed by explicit written rules rather than technical containment. I practice both, and I arrived at the second not as a philosophy adopted up front, but in response to LLMs consistently failing to follow instructions. This has been the biggest challenge I have run into by far.

When in development mode, I treat the AI agent as I would a Jr. Engineer. I retain sole authority over architecture and direction: I set the objectives and priorities, and I define what "right" looks like. Another way of saying that is leadership. An Architect or Manager might be responsible for a project where they don't know every language or technology used in the stack, but they still have to work with the Engineers that do to ensure it is built correctly. The process is similar to how I work as a [consultant](https://hbr.org/2018/07/how-consultants-project-expertise-and-learn-at-the-same-time), leaning on my own expertise while learning on the fly. A personal motivation for this project is exactly that: understanding these architectural structures generically, independent of any particular language or domain.

Other parts of the process are more collaborative. LLMs can be useful for brainstorming ideas or as sparring partners to challenge them. Sometimes the freedom to explore even bad ideas can be a worthwhile endeavor. I've found LLMs particularly helpful for developing rough drafts that articulate my ideas, which I will then refine and edit several times over. Much of the formal writing in this repository falls into this category.

Attempting to catalog and enumerate every instance of AI use would be an exercise in futility and like trying to unbake a cake. Much of the documentation was truly collaborative. I have reviewed all published documentation in this repository in detail to ensure it represents my current thinking, and I can account for what is in it. That is not a claim that I wrote every line, and it does not mean I am finished learning any of it, or that the work is complete or settled. If I'd started with the ideas fully formed then I wouldn't have needed to conduct the research. AI tooling helps me work efficiently but lacks the wisdom to ensure we are doing the right things. Regardless of who wrote or did what, I set the direction, decide what is accepted, and remain accountable for everything published.

"Management is doing things right; leadership is doing the right things." - Peter Drucker and Warren Bennis, as quoted by Stephen R. Covey in The 7 Habits of Highly Effective People.
